Fajr Lamp & Alarm

A bedside lamp and alarm clock in one: an opal glass globe on a 3D-printed base. It sleeps on an 18650 cell almost all day, then 15 minutes before your chosen prayer (Fajr by default, and the rules are written so other prayers can be added) the globe fades from deep red through amber to warm white, and your chosen recording plays at the prayer itself.

Overview

Reading the sizes: all sizes are in centimetres. Ø means diameter, the width of a circle measured straight across its middle, so “Ø16 cm” is a circle 16 cm wide. For scale: the whole lamp is about 16 cm wide and 20 cm tall, roughly the width of an A4 sheet (21 cm) in height, and the globe is a bit bigger than a grapefruit.

A Ø16 cm opal glass globe in a 3D-printed base that tapers from Ø16 cm to Ø15 cm over its 5 cm height. The globe gives an even glow with no visible LEDs. The base holds every part, carries an always-on e-paper clock on its flat front, and sends the alarm sound out through the bottom.

Before you order
  • Buy the globe first and measure its neck (outside diameter, length, any lip). The printed seat is sized from it.
  • Confirm the e-paper is in stock and is the black/white WeAct 2.13″ with the SSD1680 controller. If it’s sold out, the same module is on AliExpress from about 482 EGP (a few weeks).
  • Buy a new, genuine 18650 cell, not a used or unbranded one.
  • Pick the base colour (black PLA is the default; matte black PLA-CF costs about 155 EGP more).
  • Compare the generated Fajr times with your mosque’s timetable and note any constant difference.

How it works

An ESP32 that stays awake burns tens of milliamps and has no accurate clock. So the jobs are split: a DS3231 clock (about a minute of drift per year, per its spec) keeps time and pulls its INT line low when an event is due, while the ESP32-C3 sleeps until that line or a touch wakes it. It runs the event, programs the next one into the clock, and goes back to sleep. The ring and the MP3 board share one switched ground, so neither draws anything while it sleeps.

The daily cycle

What you can schedule

The schedule is a short list of rules. Each rule says which days, when and what to do, and you can have as many rules as you like.

When
  • A prayer ± minutes: Prayer(Fajr, 0) is Fajr itself, Prayer(Fajr, -20) 20 minutes before, Prayer(Isha, 10) 10 minutes after Isha. Fajr, Dhuhr, Asr, Maghrib and Isha all come from the precomputed Mansoura table, 2026–2045.
  • A fixed time in Egypt time, for example 07:30. Daylight saving is handled automatically.
Which days
  • Any mix of the week: every day, Sunday–Thursday, Friday only, and so on.
ActionWhat it does
Sunrise { ramp_min }The colour sunrise: deep red → amber → warm white over N minutes, finishing exactly at the rule’s time
Play { track, volume, fade_in_s }Plays /mp3/000N.mp3 from the microSD card, fading the volume up over N seconds
LampOn / LampOffTurns the lamp on or off at that time
AlarmFlashes the ring at full brightness (there is no buzzer). Prefer Play for a sound alarm.
Example schedule
const RULES: [Rule; 6] = [
    // Every day: 15-min sunrise ending at Fajr, then the adhan (track 1)
    Rule { days: ALL_DAYS,    when: When::Prayer(Prayer::Fajr, 0),  action: Action::Sunrise { ramp_min: 15 } },
    Rule { days: ALL_DAYS,    when: When::Prayer(Prayer::Fajr, 0),  action: Action::Play { track: 1, volume: 22, fade_in_s: 30 } },
    // Lamp off 10 minutes after Fajr
    Rule { days: ALL_DAYS,    when: When::Prayer(Prayer::Fajr, 10), action: Action::LampOff },
    // Sunday–Thursday: gentle wake-up at 07:30 with track 2
    Rule { days: 0b0001_1111, when: When::Local(7*60 + 30), action: Action::Sunrise { ramp_min: 10 } },
    Rule { days: 0b0001_1111, when: When::Local(7*60 + 30), action: Action::Play { track: 2, volume: 15, fade_in_s: 60 } },
    // Friday: lamp on at 09:00
    Rule { days: 1 << 5,      when: When::Local(9*60),  action: Action::LampOn },
];
// days: bit 0 = Sunday … bit 6 = Saturday

Buttons and menu

The touch pad is the only thing that wakes the lamp. The four buttons are on the right-hand side, towards the back, and are read through the PCF8574 only while the lamp is awake: tap the pad, then press a button within ten seconds, or the lamp goes back to sleep. A tap shows the standby face and lights the globe at 5% so you can read it. A hold of one second turns the lamp on or off, and any touch stops an alarm that is ringing. OK on the standby face opens the menu; Back steps out one level and, on the standby face, does nothing.

Keeping it upright
  • Fill the 4 cups (Ø2.5 × 0.8 cm) with loose lead shot. The four cups hold 15.7 cm³ in all, which is about 100 g of shot (178 g only if they were solid lead), or about 75 g if you use twelve M12 washers, three per cup.
  • With a 0.6 kg globe and 175 g of lead, the lamp tips at about 46° and takes about 390 g of sideways push at the top of the globe. Without ballast it's about 42° and 330 g. That's typical for a table lamp.
  • The TPU feet sit near the edge and stop it sliding.
  • No glue on the globe. If you want it permanent, use 3 dots of clear neutral-cure silicone, never epoxy or superglue.
Measure the globe before ordering The seat and grip ring assume an 8 cm neck, which is a guess. When the globe arrives, measure the neck's outside diameter and length, check whether it has a lip, and weigh it. The Amazon listing's 4.5 kg is almost certainly the shipping weight. Then regenerate the files with python base.py round <neck mm> (the script takes millimetres, so an 8 cm neck is 80). If the neck has a lip, the collar can take 3 nylon M4 thumbscrews instead of the grip ring.

No printer nearby? A wood turner can make the same shape from beech with 0.6 cm walls, though you’d fit the rails and frames by hand.

Wiring

Mistakes that ruin the build
  • Never connect the battery to the 3V3 pin: 4.2 V is above its limit. Using 5V goes through the on-board LDO.
  • You must clear the DS3231 A1F flag before sleeping. If INT is still low, the chip refuses to sleep (noted in the esp-hal 1.2 release notes) or wakes immediately.
  • The TTP223 calibrates itself at power-up. Don't touch the pad while the battery goes in.
  • Set the DS3231 to UTC, not Egypt time. That is what lets daylight-saving changes pass without any intervention.
  • Take the cell off the ESP32 before plugging its USB-C in to flash. No source I found says whether the SuperMini's 5V pin has a diode, so assume the cell and USB are tied together.
  • The DS3231 board is a ZS-042 made for a rechargeable coin cell. With a CR2032 in it, remove the charge resistor (a small SMD next to the diode, usually marked 201) and the POWER LED. Keep the I²C pull-ups.
  • Remove the red power LED from the SuperMini. With it lit, deep sleep draws far more than the 43 µA the board is quoted at.
  • Don't sleep while the touch pad is still held: the pin is low, so the chip wakes straight away. Wait for release first.

Sound: play your own audio

The alarm plays an MP3 you choose, such as an adhan recording at the prayer you pick and a softer track at 07:30. The MP3 board reads the file from a microSD card and drives the speaker. The ESP32 only sends it short serial commands like "volume 5" and "play track 1", so the audio never has to fit in the chip's flash.

How it plays
  1. The rail is already on for the light ramp, so the board has had minutes to start up before the prayer.
  2. Set volume 5, play /mp3/0001.mp3, then raise the volume one step every few seconds over a minute.
  3. A touch stops it. If the board never answers, the ring flashes at full brightness instead.
  4. Before sleeping, turn the rail off and leave GPIO21 floating.
Watch out for
  • Low battery: the board is meant for 4.5–5 V. Near empty (about 3.4 V) it may get quiet or reset. If that happens, add a small 5 V boost board on the same switched line.
  • File names: use a folder named mp3 and four-digit names (0001.mp3, 0002.mp3). Copying files in a different order won't change which one plays.
  • Volume: the 0.6 cm gap under the feet is the speaker's only outlet. Don't put the lamp on a cloth or a soft surface.
core/src/lib.rs (tested on the computer)
// Every command is a 10-byte frame: 7E FF 06 CMD 00 PH PL CK_H CK_L EF
pub fn frame(cmd: u8, param: u16) -> [u8; 10] {
    let [ph, pl] = param.to_be_bytes();
    let sum = 0xFFu16 + 0x06 + cmd as u16 + ph as u16 + pl as u16;
    let [ch, cl] = 0u16.wrapping_sub(sum).to_be_bytes();
    [0x7E, 0xFF, 0x06, cmd, 0x00, ph, pl, ch, cl, 0xEF]
}
// test: frame(0x03, 1) == 7E FF 06 03 00 00 01 FE F7 EF  (the commonly published "play track 1")

Software

Prayer-time table for years ahead

All five prayers are built. A rule picks its trigger with When::Prayer(prayer, minutes) or a fixed clock time, and you can have several rules at once. The generator prayer-gen computes Fajr (19.5°), Dhuhr, Asr (standard shadow), Maghrib (sunset) and Isha (17.5°), the Egyptian General Authority of Survey method, from 2026 to 2045 and writes a 73,050-byte file: five 16-bit numbers per day, in that order. The table is in UTC, so it is unaffected by daylight saving or a law change. DST only matters for display and for rules you write in local time; that function is tested on 2026 and 2027 (this year it ends Thursday 29 October 2026 at 24:00 Egypt time). Maghrib is the moment the sun's upper edge reaches the horizon (−0.833°), not a fixed number of minutes after.

Compared with the Aladhan calculator for Cairo (method 5, standard Asr) on all 122 days of January, March, June and September 2026. Asr drifts with the season (about 1 to 2 minutes later in winter, 1 minute earlier in September) while the other four stay within a minute; I did not find which calculator is the more accurate for Asr, so that prayer is the one to compare with your mosque. Every time is rounded up to the next whole minute, so none is earlier than the astronomical value. Official timetables add safety margins, so compare with your mosque's or Dar al-Ifta's and tell me any constant difference.

The code

Rust all the way Every driver in the final build has a Rust path. The ring uses the C3's RMT peripheral (the esp-hal-smartled crate). The SSD1680 e-paper uses the ssd1680 or epd-waveshare crate with partial refresh, plus a full refresh once an hour to clear ghosting. The DS3231 and MP3 commands are already written and tested in core. The DS3231's second alarm can wake the chip every minute to redraw the clock. I haven't checked those crates' versions against esp-hal 1.2, so pin whichever versions compile together.

The Rust project is in the firmware_ws/ folder next to this page and has two parts so far: core (the schedule over five prayers and own alarms, DST, clock registers, MP3 frames, the ramp curve, the stored settings, the menu, the button decoding and the INA219 reading: 37 unit tests) and prayer-gen (table generator). The firmware crate (esp-hal) is not written yet; the ring, e-paper, flash storage, button reading and minute-redraw code come with stages 1 and 3. Your schedule lives in one constant (see What you can schedule):

The full wake-then-sleep cycle (esp-hal skeleton, untested)

Tested on a computer: next_event, alarm1_registers, parse_time_registers and ramp_duty. Untested: the esp-hal calls and the colour mapping. The 1.2 release changed the sleep API (a pin you listen(Event::LowLevel) on is the wake source, and RtcioWakeupSource is gone), so expect small fixes to paths and names. The touch pad on GPIO4 is set up the same way as GPIO3 below.

firmware/src/main.rs (excerpt)
// 1) Read time and status from the DS3231 (UTC)
i2c.write_read(I2C_ADDR, &[ds3231::REG_TIME], &mut t)?;
let now_min = ds3231::parse_time_registers(t).div_euclid(60);

// 2) If the alarm woke us, run the event that just came due
if let Some(ev) = next_event(now_min - 2, &RULES, &prayers) {
    if ev.utc_min <= now_min { run(ev.action); }
}

// 3) Clear A1F, otherwise INT stays low and sleep is refused
i2c.write(I2C_ADDR, &[ds3231::REG_STATUS, status & !ds3231::STATUS_A1F])?;

// 4) Program the next event (date + hour + minute) and enable Alarm 1
let a = ds3231::alarm1_registers(next_event(now_min, &RULES, &prayers)?.utc_min);
i2c.write(I2C_ADDR, &[ds3231::REG_ALARM1, a[0], a[1], a[2], a[3]])?;
i2c.write(I2C_ADDR, &[ds3231::REG_CONTROL, ds3231::CONTROL_ALARM1_ONLY])?;

// 5) Pins wake on low level, then deep sleep
let mut int = Input::new(p.GPIO3, InputConfig::default().with_pull(Pull::Up));
int.apply_wakeup_config(&WakeupConfig::default().with_low_power_path(true))?;
int.listen(Event::LowLevel);
LowPower::new(p.LPWR).sleep_deep(RtcSleepConfig::deep());

Before the DS3231 is powered

A CR2032 cannot be recharged, and boards of this type have a small resistor-and-diode path that charges the cell. On this one it is most likely R4 with D2, at the top left under the SCL hole, but the resistor’s value is not readable in the photo, so nothing has been removed. Until that is confirmed, take the coin cell out before powering the board. Power it from 3V3 only: the I²C pull-ups go to VCC, so 5 V would put 5 V on pins the ESP32-C3 cannot take.

What this changed in the design

  • I²C pins are free in esp-hal. GPIO6 and GPIO7 from the pin map stand. The Arduino default of GPIO8 and GPIO9 does not apply, and GPIO8 is also the onboard LED.
  • Wi-Fi is not part of the design yet. The lamp sleeps with the radio off. NTP is a way to set the DS3231 to UTC while developing. Using Wi-Fi in the finished lamp would add to the battery budget in the calculator below.
  • Generating the project: with esp-generate 2.0.0 the chip is chosen with -o esp32c3, not --chip.

Suggested next steps

  1. Make the DS3231 safe. Take the CR2032 out. Photograph the R4 and D2 area close up and confirm which part is the charging resistor before it is removed; milestone 1 part A needs the charge resistor and LED off anyway.
  2. Decide how to connect it. Check whether the SuperMini has header pins. The DS3231 has male pins, so it needs male pins on the SuperMini and female-to-female jumpers, or a breadboard. Wire VCC to 3V3, GND, SDA to GPIO6 and SCL to GPIO7, and leave SQW unconnected for now.
  3. Talk to it from wifi-ntp/. Scan the I²C bus (expect 0x68, and probably 0x57 for the memory), read the time with parse_time_registers, write the NTP time with time_registers, and read it back.
  4. Test the battery. Once the charging path is dealt with and the cell is refitted, unplug USB for 30 seconds, plug it back in and check that the time kept running.
  5. Identify the display. Photograph both sides and the printed text on its cable, confirm the SSD1680, then follow the milestone 3 gate (8-pad header, reset, busy and data/command pins, the weact-studio-epd crate).
  6. Join the pieces. Take the alarm time from core’s schedule, compare it with the DS3231, and start the ramp. Then move the working code out of the scratch projects into a lamp crate in firmware_ws/ that depends on lamp-core.

Battery calculator

The defaults are my assumptions, not measurements: measure the real sleep current with a multimeter on the positive wire before trusting the result. The result assumes 80% of the cell's capacity is usable, because the ring needs at least 3.5 V and the chip browns out near the same point.

23 daysbetween charges

The lamp is what drains the battery. Sleeping with a once-a-minute clock redraw is assumed to average 1 mA until you measure it (the DS3231 alone is about 0.2 mA, and each redraw wakes the chip), which would last about 3.7 months. Each 10 minutes of the ring at its 300 mA cap costs about 52 mAh, which is about 2 days of that sleep. At the defaults the lamp needs charging about every 3 weeks; measure the background current in milestone 1 and replace the default. For longer life, lower the cap or shorten the time the lamp stays on after the prayer.

Still to check

Tested so far, on a computer only: the schedule logic, Egypt daylight saving, the DS3231 register encoding, the ramp and the MP3 commands (18 unit tests), and Fajr times within a minute of the Aladhan calculator on 122 days of 2026 (not yet against a mosque timetable).

  • Firmware: the logic (schedule, five prayers, menu, stored settings, button and battery decoding) is tested on the PC, and parts of it now run on the board (the LED ramp from core, Wi-Fi, NTP and the UTC-to-Cairo conversion; see the build log), but the lamp’s own esp-hal crate is not written. The ring, e-paper drawing, flash storage, button polling and minute-redraw code isn’t written yet.
  • Low battery voltage: the ring is rated from 3.5 V and the MP3 board recommends 4.5–5 V, but the cell runs 3.2–4.2 V. Milestones 1 and 2 test this; the fallback is a 5 V boost board.
  • Switched ground: never built. Check that nothing leaks through the ring or player pins while their ground is off.
  • Sleep current: an estimate until measured in milestone 1. The calculator assumes 1 mA.
  • Battery level and buttons: the INA219 (battery) and PCF8574 (four buttons) share the DS3231’s I²C wires; nothing about them has been run. Check with an I²C scan that 0x68, 0x40 and the expander’s address do not clash, that the modules have no power LED or extra pull-ups to the wrong voltage, and that their sleep draw fits the budget. A cell voltage is only a rough percentage and must be read with the ring and player off. Where the four buttons go in the base is not yet in the CAD, and the menu and its layout are not written.
  • The base: never test-printed, and the globe seat assumes an 8 cm neck until you measure yours.
  • Fajr times: official timetables add safety margins. Set the offset to match your mosque’s timetable.
