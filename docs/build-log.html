Build log

The record of what has actually been run on the real board, oldest first. It is history, not the plan: what comes next is in the roadmap. Where the build stands as a whole is in STATUS.md. The scratch projects live in scratch/ (blink-test/, wifi-ntp/) and are experiments, not the lamp firmware.

USB and flashingPassed · 28 Octespflash board-info reads an ESP32-C3 (revision v0.4) with 4 MB flash over the board’s own USB-C port, no extra chip. On Arch the user must be in the uucp group to open /dev/ttyACM0. Before the first flash the factory firmware reset the chip every 2–3 seconds (the USB device kept reconnecting); flashing our own program stopped it.
Blink and PWMPassedThe onboard LED is on GPIO8 and is active-low. LEDC PWM (24 kHz, 8-bit) fades it smoothly. The ring will use the RMT peripheral instead, so this was only a stand-in for watching the ramp.
core on the chipPassedlamp-core is no_std and builds for the C3 as a path dependency. ramp_duty drove the LED through a 30-second test ramp.
Wi-Fi, DHCP, DNS, NTP (scratch/wifi-ntp/)PassedJoined the router (WPA2), got an IP address, resolved pool.ntp.org and read the NTP reply. Notes: esp-radio is 1.0.0-beta.1 with no stability guarantee; it does not support WPA3 or 5 GHz; the generator needs the wifi, alloc, unstable-hal and embassy options; and embassy-net needs the dns feature added by hand in the generated Cargo.toml.
Cairo local time through corePassedThe UTC minutes from the NTP reply were fed into utc_to_egypt and the board printed the Cairo date and time at UTC+2, as expected after summer time ended at midnight on 29 Oct.
DS3231 on the busPassed · 3 NovA blue HW-084 board with a 6-pin header (32K SQW SCL SDA VCC GND): VCC from the SuperMini's 3V3 pin, 3.29 V measured at the pin, SDA on GPIO6 and SCL on GPIO7. An address scan finds 0x68 (the clock) and 0x57 (the AT24C32 memory on the same board). The coin cell is out while the charge-resistor question stays open, so the time is lost whenever the board is unplugged.
DS3231 set to UTCPassed · 3 NovSeven BCD registers written in one 8-byte transaction (pointer 0x00, then seconds to year) from the NTP Unix seconds; read back in the same second as 2026-11-03 00:55:02 UTC, which is 02:55:02 in Cairo, and the seconds advance. CONTROL was left untouched, so no alarm bit is armed yet. NTP depends on which pool member answers: pool.ntp.org handed out 197.224.66.40, which never replies, while time.google.com does. The code still tries only addrs[0].
DisplayPassed · 4 NovA WeAct 2.13″ module, 250 × 122, black and white, header labelled BUSY RES D/C CS SCL SDA GND VCC, left on its factory 4-line SPI bridges. Wired as the pin map says: RES→GPIO0, BUSY→GPIO1, D/C→GPIO2, SDA→GPIO8, SCL→GPIO9, CS→GND, plus 3V3 and GND. weact-studio-epd in blocking mode, with a small write-only SPI shim in src/display.rs because the chip-select is tied low and there is no SPI-device pin to give the driver. The panel shows a frame we wrote ourselves — wakecue / display works / 250 × 122 — does a full refresh in about two seconds, then is put back to sleep. Not done yet: partial refresh, and the sleep current with the panel fitted.
Alarm 1 pulls a pin lowPassed · 3 NovAlarm 1 programmed two minutes ahead, A1F cleared first, then CONTROL_ALARM1_ONLY written: bit 0 of STATUS set at the target minute, with the seconds field at 0 as the helper intends. With SQW on GPIO3 and only the chip’s internal pull-up (no resistor on the bench), the pin read 3.3 V idle and 0.02 V while the flag was set, and climbed back to 3.3 V once A1F was cleared. The pin is open-drain, so low means “an alarm is due”, which is the wake signal.
Deep sleep, woken by the clockPassed · 3 Novapply_wakeup_config with the low-power path plus listen(Event::LowLevel) on GPIO3, then LowPower::sleep_deep. The USB-Serial-JTAG peripheral powers down, so espflash ends with “no serial ports” or “broken pipe” — that is the sleep, not a fault. The port came back on the alarm two minutes later, the board re-synced time and re-armed, and the cycle repeated. This is milestone 1’s architecture: the chip is off when the event is not due.
Touch pad polarityMeasured · 3 NovThe TTP223 alone on the breadboard: VCC and GND on the rails, OUT on the meter, nothing connected to the ESP32. Idle read 0 V and a finger on the pad read 3.29 V, so the module is at its factory default, active high, with no jumper to change it. There are no A/B pads anywhere on this board, front or back, so the output cannot be flipped: it is fixed active high. The design assumed the jumper version, so GPIO4 is armed on a high level instead of a low one; idling at 0 V is then the non-wake state, which is what sleep needs. That level is the only thing that changes: the pin still takes no pull-up, and the pad must still be released before the chip goes back to sleep.
Deep sleep, woken by the touch padPassed · 3 NovOUT to GPIO4 with no pull-up, armed with apply_wakeup_config (low-power path) and listen(Event::HighLevel) — the opposite level to the clock, which is the only level this module can give. With Alarm 1 moved an hour out so the clock could not be the wake source, the LED went out (asleep, USB port gone) and a touch brought the LED and the port back. A guard before sleep_deep holds the board awake while either wake pin still sits at its wake level, so a finger left on the pad only blinks the pin that is holding it instead of starting a sleep that ends at once; the chip slept as soon as the pad was released. Putting the alarm back to two minutes then showed both sources armed together — the clock woke the same build through GPIO3 with GPIO4 still armed high, and the chip re-slept. One false lead recorded: a shell loop that reflashed every 2 s reset the chip before it could reach the sleep call, and the lit LED then belonged to the loop, not the firmware.
Alarm cleared with no networkPassed · 4 NovClearing A1F and programming the next alarm used to sit inside the branch that only runs when NTP succeeds. A boot that woke with no network therefore left A1F set — which holds SQW low — and the pre-sleep guard waited on a level that nothing was left to clear: the board stayed awake for good. Only setting the time needs the network now; the flag and the next alarm are handled on whatever time the clock already holds, so a board with no Wi-Fi sleeps instead of staying lit. Seen on the board, accidentally and usefully: builds carrying placeholder credentials never reached the network and still went to sleep.
Network phase has a deadlineWritten, not yet seen firing · 4 NovWi-Fi connect, the DHCP wait and the DNS lookup were awaited with no limit, and a failed association fell through to a DHCP wait that could never finish: the board sat lit with the USB port up and never reached the sleep step. That stall was seen, not guessed. The whole exchange now runs inside a 30 s timeout where any failure means “no fresh time” and the alarm work runs anyway. The timeout reaching 30 s has not been observed.
Clock's power-loss flag (OSF)Passed · 4 NovBoot reads STATUS before anything else, shows a two-second dark pause when bit 7 is set, and clears that bit only after a fresh time was written — reading STATUS back to prove the chip accepted it. On the board: the pause appears after unplugging (no coin cell, so the clock really does lose power) and does not appear after a flash, which resets the chip while keeping power, so the clear survives a reset. With the flag clear the radio is never started: that boot sleeps in a fraction of a second, against about four seconds for a boot that has to fetch a time.
Four menu buttons on the expanderPassed · 4 NovThe expander answers at 0x20 next to the clock at 0x68 and the memory at 0x57, so all three boards sit on GPIO6 and GPIO7 together. The bench binary releases all eight pins (writes 0xFF) and reads the port every 20 ms: at rest every bit reads 1, and a press pulls its own bit to 0, so Up, Down, OK and Back are P0, P1, P2 and P3 and each press printed its own name. A key is reported once, after the same reading twice in a row, so a bounce is dropped and a held button does not repeat. This bench stays awake for the whole test; the lamp only reads the port after the pad has woken it.
The lamp's own boot cyclePassed · 4 Novfirmware_ws/wakecue ran its real boot path on the board, not a bench binary: draw a frame, read STATUS, read the time, ask core for the next event, clear A1F, program Alarm 1, arm GPIO3 low and GPIO4 high, sleep. The first attempt looked healthy in the log and never woke — SQW had not been reconnected after the display was rewired, so the boot armed a wake source with nothing on it. espflash waiting on “serial port disconnected” was the only visible sign, and the panel refresh is what tells a wake from a dead sleep. With the wire back the chip slept, the port stayed gone, and about a minute later it woke by itself: the panel refreshed, the boot logged again, re-armed the following minute and slept. That run read 2000-01-01 with OSF set, because fitting the wire meant unplugging the board and the clock has no cell; the untrusted-time branch woke it once a minute by design instead of parking it on a wrong clock.
Scheduled from a real prayer tablePassed · 4 Novprayer-gen wrote ten years of Cairo times (2026-01-01 .. 2035-12-31, 3652 days) and the firmware embeds the file. The day range travels in the file’s own header and PrayerTable::from_blob reads it back, so a table regenerated over another range cannot be paired with a stale first day — that mistake would have looked up the right prayer on the wrong day and shown no symptom at all. On the board: boot: osf false, now 2026-11-04 23:16 UTC = 2026-11-05 01:16 Cairo, then next: Sunrise { ramp_min: 15 } at 2026-11-05 02:29 UTC = 04:29 Cairo (193 min from now), then it slept. Fajr that morning is 04:44 Cairo, so 04:29 is the ramp starting fifteen minutes early to finish at the prayer. This is the first sleep measured in hours instead of one minute. Log times print as dates in UTC and Cairo (format_minutes in core, a 16-byte buffer, no allocation); the 04:29 wake itself has not been watched yet.
The face shows the clockPassed · 5 NovThe panel stopped showing the test frame: every wake now draws the Cairo time, the date and the event the boot just armed, all read from the clock after the schedule is computed. format_hhmm and format_date in core do the text, tested on the PC with a leap day, 2099-12-31 and the +2/+3 switch. Two layout lessons: the driver's buffer is the glass on its short edge — 128 wide by 250 tall, six columns hidden behind the border — so with no rotation the face comes out portrait, and DisplayRotation::Rotate90 makes the drawing area 250 by 128, matching the window in the base. One catch worth writing down: the face is only right at the moment of a wake, because a sleeping panel keeps the image it was last given; between wakes it shows an old minute.
DEV_MODE keeps the board awakePassed · 5 NovA sleeping board takes the USB port and the log with it, so every boot was a race and every flash started in the ROM bootloader. With DEV_MODE true the boot runs for real — clock, schedule, alarm, face — and then stays awake as a console: button presses, the pad, and the clock's own answer every five seconds, so a stopped clock shows up while sitting at the bench instead of at 4 a.m. The boot line says dev mode true, so a build can never be mistaken for the final one. It also found a flashing trap: --after watchdog-reset cannot be paired with --monitor, because the app’s USB-Serial-JTAG re-enumerates and the monitor dies holding a dead port, ending in I/O error after a flash that succeeded.
A clock that wakes every minutePassed · 5 NovWith DEV_MODE false the lamp sleeps and comes back by itself on each new minute, which is the first time the panel has been asked to keep its RAM across a deep sleep — the assumption the fast redraw rests on. What that costs, in arithmetic rather than measurement: the chip draws roughly 20 mA awake and is awake well under a second per wake (boot, one clock read, one fast refresh), so the redraw averages something near 0.3 mA. That is cheaper than the 1 mA the battery calculator had been assuming, and far cheaper than the two power LEDs still fitted at about 1 mA each, so the hardware gates still matter more than the minute wakes. It lands exactly on milestone 1 part A: gate (c) measures the sleeping board, gate (d) measures the average over ten minutes with the wakes included, and until those two numbers exist the 23-day figure is a guess.
A face that reads like a clockPassed · 5 NovThe face switched to twelve-hour time — 4:29 PM, with the meridiem beside the digits in the small font, and Dhuhr 11:39 AM on the prayer line — and gained a corner mark, z z z, saying the lamp is down. The mark is part of the frame rather than something erased later: a minute tick redraws the face whole so it keeps it, a wake by hand draws the face without it, and the lamp puts it back just before it sleeps. Midnight and noon both read 12, which is the case that usually breaks: 12:00 AM and 12:00 PM, tested on the PC along with 9:05 and 23:59. The log deliberately stays on 24-hour stamps, which need no meridiem to be unambiguous.
Any button wakes the lampPassed · 5 NovThe expander’s INT now shares GPIO3 with the clock’s SQW. Both are open-drain, so neither can drive the line high and the one pull-up serves both: a wake with A1F set was the clock, a wake with it clear was a hand — and the firmware never needs to know whether that hand was on the pad or on a button. One trap came with it: INT stays latched low after a press until the expander’s port is read, so the pre-sleep wait now reads the port as it waits, or a press landing there would have pinned the lamp awake for good.
The face shows today's five prayersDrawn, not yet read · 6 NovThe standby face grew the strip from the mock-up below: the five prayers in a row, each with its time and a dot that is filled when that prayer rings. The dots read the switches from the stored block, the times come from the prayer table by the local date, and the countdown to the next prayer is held against the right edge so a tomorrow on the same line cannot collide with it. The layout is also the measurement now: every mark stays six pixels inside the drawing area, because which six rows or columns the panel hides is still unmeasured, and the menu's border at the very edge is what answers that. The strip has been part of every face since, so it is no longer unseen; what nobody has read back yet is whether the five times and their dots say what the switches say.
An alarm takes the lamp overPassed · 6 NovThe due-event check used to write a log line and move on. Now a due prayer sound or a user alarm takes the whole panel: the name (or Alarm) and the time it was due, on alternating white and black frames so the glass flashes about once a second, until a key or a touch answers it, or a minute passes and the lamp gives up and goes back to sleep. It obeys the stored rules, so an alarm that fires is itself proof the block was read. Two things had to change with it: Alarm 1 is now armed after the alarm window rather than before it, because a minute that has already gone by matches nothing and would leave the lamp asleep for good; and the Top screen lost its Exit row, since Back is the way out. Nothing rings yet — the ring and the card hang off the same call, with the same title and the same deadline. The home face names the alarm in its Next line when it comes before the next prayer, and re-reads that from the settings on every draw: an alarm set from the menu would otherwise not appear until the next boot, because the line was decided once at boot and a boot is a whole wake. Seen on the board, 6 Nov: an alarm set two minutes out on every day was named on the sleeping face, and the boot that landed on its minute took the whole panel and flashed until it was answered, then put the clock back.
The menu keeps up with the buttonsPassed · 6 NovReported as “sometimes I click and nothing happens, then one works”, and the log answered it: every press redrew the panel and waited, and panel: drew in 778 ms is the size of the blind spot in which presses were never read. Two changes fixed it. A run of presses is now applied to the menu first and drawn once when the fingers stop, so five Down presses move five rows and cost one refresh instead of one row and five blind windows. And the refresh itself is started and finished separately in the vendored driver, so the loop keeps reading the expander at 50 Hz while the glass works: input is never lost, the glass is simply a little behind, and a press that lands mid-refresh is applied then and shown when the panel next draws. The alarm got the same benefit — it polls between frames, so answering it takes about 50 ms rather than most of a second.
The lamp computes the prayer timesTested on the PC, not yet seen · 6 NovThe ten-year Cairo table left the image: core::astro works out the five times from the place and method in the stored block on every boot, with libm supplying the trigonometry core does not have. The clock those times are read against is still Egypt's, summer time and all, so this step moves the place and leaves the zone: a lamp set outside Egypt would compute the right sun and then show it on the wrong clock. The method is the stored one — Fajr's angle, Isha as an angle or as minutes after Maghrib, standard or Hanafi Asr — and a place where the sun never reaches the angle (a polar summer) gets no time rather than an invented one; the block's high_lat rule is stored and nothing reads it yet. The old table is not thrown away: it is core/tests/data/prayers-cairo.bin, and a test asks the new code for all 18,260 of its times, so the arithmetic is held against ten years of what the glass has been showing rather than against itself. The image is 153 KB against the 1 MB application partition: 36 KB of table out, about 13 KB of libm in, the rest what the panel, the menu and the alarms have added since the first frame. The gate is the strip: the same five times on the glass, which is one flash and one look.
The wall clock follows the stored zonePassed · 8 Novcore::calendar::Zone (winter offset plus a none, egypt, europe or us rule) does every conversion between the clock's UTC and the wall, so the face, the alarms and the prayer strip no longer assume Egypt. The new Time zone screen (top menu, third row) sets the hour (-12 to +14), the minute (00, 15, 30, 45) and the rule, and writes them only on Save. Checked on the board: the screen draws and takes the four buttons, a saved zone moves the face to that zone's time at once, and it survives an unplug. A bug found on the way: after a menu change the face was redrawn from the boot copy of the settings, so a saved change only showed on the next minute's wake; it now redraws from the menu's own copy. Offsets between -0:59 and 0 cannot be built on the screen (no zone is there).
The lamp's own network and DHCPPassed · 8 NovThe roadmap's trip-wire for move 1: can a real phone get an address from the lamp's own DHCP server. The top menu's Phone setup row starts the radio inside the real app (firmware_ws/wakecue, on esp-hal and esp-radio), raises an open network Wakecue-XXXX (the last two bytes of the access point's MAC) at 192.168.4.1 and answers on UDP port 67 from core::dhcp, a small DHCP server (discover to offer, request to ack or nak, eight leases in RAM, 12 tests on the PC). A phone joined the network and was given .2, the first address of the pool. The normal wake-and-sleep path never starts the radio; Back or a touch resets into it. The app image grew from 160 KB to 545 KB. Nothing is served on top yet: no page, no DNS, so a browser has nothing to open at 192.168.4.1. The first attempt put the access point in scratch/wifi-ntp by mistake; it was moved into the app.
The setup page on the lamp's own networkPassed · 8 NovOn the Wakecue-XXXX network, DNS answers every name with 192.168.4.1 (core::dns), and a web server on port 80 serves one page (core::http, core::setup_page, routed by core::portal): the Wi-Fi name and password, the UTC offset, the summer-time rule and the place (name, latitude, longitude). The phone connectivity checks are answered with a redirect to the page, which is what makes a phone offer to sign in. The parsing and routing is pure and was tested on the PC (176 tests, including every route and the rule that the saved password is never put in a page, since the network is open). An Android phone offered to sign in by itself as soon as it joined, which opened the page; the form worked and the saved zone was still there afterwards. The form is applied to a copy, and only a form that passed every check is written to flash, the way a menu change is. The app image is 575 KB. Saving the Wi-Fi name and password does not make the lamp join that network yet. One connection is served at a time, and a second one that arrives meanwhile is refused and retried.
The clock set from the owner's networkPassed · 8 NovOn a boot where the DS3231 reports it lost power (OSF, which an unplug with no coin cell causes) and a Wi-Fi name is stored, the lamp joins that network (wakecue/src/station.rs), asks time.google.com for the time (core::ntp: a version 3 client packet, the answer refused if it is short, not a server's, stratum 0, unsynchronised or before 2025), writes the clock, clears OSF and A1F, records setup.wifi and setup.clock in the settings block, and resets into a clean boot. It synced. The whole exchange has a 30 s deadline and every failure inside it falls back to the one-minute wake. The first attempt panicked in the timer queue: the sync polls the network by hand before any executor exists, and embassy-time's integrated queue rejects a waker the Embassy executor did not create. Enabling its generic queue (16 slots) fixed it, and that change also applies to the setup mode, which was seen before it. The radio is started once per boot through radio::Lazy and shared between this and the setup mode.
Sync message and first-boot setupPassed · 8 NovWhile the clock is fetched the panel shows Syncing time... with the network's name under it; the face is taken over before the sync for that. With nothing stored in the settings block the lamp raises its Wakecue-XXXX network by itself instead of waiting for the menu. Skipping that setup with Back or a touch (which writes the settings as they stand, with the place marked as decided) has not been tried yet.
Place from a list, prayer-time rounding and calculation methodPassed · 8 NovThe setup page's city search (core::cities, 440 places, sent from flash at /cities) filled in the place, the coordinates, the offset and the summer-time rule, and worked from the phone. Two comparisons against published times then found two faults, both fixed and both seen to be fixed: the lamp rounded every time up where the Egyptian Survey Authority's table rounds to the nearest minute (measured on the Authority's own Mansoura table: round up reproduces 43% of its 760 published times, nearest 94%; fixed in core::astro and pinned by tests/egas_mansoura.rs), and it applied the Egyptian convention in every country (Makkah's Fajr read 5:04 where Umm al-Qura and Google give 5:09). The page now has a Calculation method box with 19 authorities (core::methods, numbers from AlAdhan's method list and four methods' own minute shifts measured from its output), filled in with a suggestion for the place picked, and the chosen id is kept in the settings block. The arithmetic with each method's numbers was compared with AlAdhan for all 19 methods on four dates before it was trusted. Not done, and written down: Isha 120 minutes in Ramadan for Umm al-Qura, a high-latitude rule in summer above about 48 degrees north, and Iran's method.
The MP3 board over serialPassed · 8 NovA DFPlayer Mini HW-247A V0.5.1 with no letters printed on it, so its pins were named by position from DFRobot's datasheet picture (held microSD slot up, notch at the top; VCC is the top-left pad). The first try had the board mounted the wrong way round, every wire landed on the wrong pad and the SuperMini died when USB went in; turned the right way it worked with the speaker (8 Ω, measured) straight on SPK1 and SPK2 and power from the SuperMini's 5V pin, even at volume 20 of 30. Serial is UART1, 9600 baud, GPIO21 through 1 kΩ to the board's RX and the board's TX through 1 kΩ to GPIO20 (wakecue/src/bin/sound.rs, numbering.rs). What the board answered: 0x03 plays by position and 0x06 sets the volume; the card's file count is 0x48 (22 with the test beep) while 0x47 and 0x49 answer 0, the opposite of the datasheet's table; 0x12 plays /mp3/0101.mp3 by the number in its name across a gap from 0022 to 0100, so the card can number adhan 0001-0099 and alarm sounds from 0101; 0x42 answered 0x0201 while playing; at the end of a track the board sends 0x3D twice with the track's position (22 for the beep played as file 101); 0x4B, the current track, always answered 0 and is no use. After a reset of the SuperMini the board sent a stray error frame (0x40) once; the firmware must ignore frames it does not expect. Not measured yet: the low cell voltage, the switched rail, and the idle current. The SuperMini's pin rows in the wiring diagram on the hardware page are the wrong way round (5V, GND and 3V3 are on the right when the USB-C is at the top).

The MP3 board and speaker at low voltagePassed by ear · 10 NovThe player and speaker were powered from the LM2596 module (set with its trimmer, output chosen on its display) instead of the SuperMini's 5V pin, with just sound running, at 4.2 V, 3.5 V and 3.2 V. The LED ring in the appReported working · 11 NovThe 16-pixel ring on GPIO10 (through 330 Ω to DIN, DOUT unused) is driven from the lamp itself, powered all the time with no rail switch. Each wake asks schedule::active_ramp how far into a light ramp it is and sets every pixel to that step of the ramp, capped at 64 of 255; an alarm lights the end-of-ramp warm white while it rings and the ring goes dark when it ends; any other wake sends black. The pixels hold their colour through the sleep. The logic is tested on the PC (256 tests); the owner reported the ring working. Not reported: the individual ramp steps, any flicker while the chip sleeps, and the ring at 3.2-3.5 V.

The rail switchReported working · 12 NovThe AO3400 (SOT-23) was soldered dead-bug with thin wires and checked with the multimeter before it went in: the gate read open to both other legs, and the source-to-drain body diode read about 0.4 to 0.7 V one way and open the other. One thing found on the way: drain to source first beeped in both directions, because the floating gate had picked up charge and turned the channel on; touching the gate to the source cleared it, and the part was fine. Wired as designed: GPIO5 through 1 kΩ to the gate, 100 kΩ from the gate to ground, the source on the lamp's ground, and the ring's and the MP3 board's grounds joined on the drain. The ring and the MP3 board are powered from the LM2596 at 3.2 V (5 V 1 A charger in); the ESP32 is on USB. wakecue drove GPIO5 high at the start of every wake and low before the sleep. The owner reported that it works. Heard with it: a small click from the speaker on every one-minute wake, which is the MP3 board powering up. Not reported: the current with the rail off, the ring's colours at 3.2 V, and any leakage through the ring's data pin or the player's serial pins while their ground is off.

Before a coin cell goes back in

The board is powered from 3V3 only, and must stay that way: the I²C pull-ups go to VCC, so 5 V would put 5 V on pins the ESP32-C3 cannot take. A CR2032 cannot be recharged, and boards of this type carry a resistor-and-diode path that charges the cell — on this one, most likely R4 with D2 at the top left under the SCL hole. Nothing has been removed and nothing has to be while the cell sits in a drawer: at 3.3 V VCC the path has almost no headroom (3.3 V minus the diode drop is below a CR2032’s 3.0 V), so the cell will hold its charge. The choice was made on 5 Nov: remove that resistor and fit a disposable CR2032. The clock draws only a few microamps from a cell, so it will keep time for years, and lifting the resistor is what makes a non-rechargeable cell safe. Until the resistor is out, no cell goes back in. The power LED on the clock board is a separate matter: it is a steady milliamp and belongs to the sleep-current gate.

What this changed in the design

  • I²C pins are free in esp-hal. GPIO6 and GPIO7 from the pin map stand. The Arduino default of GPIO8 and GPIO9 does not apply, and GPIO8 is also the onboard LED.
  • The radio is part of the product now, and the rule it earned still stands. A lamp that leaves this bench has to be given a network, a time zone and a place by the person who owns it, and there is no keyboard on the panel and no PC in the loop once it sits on a bedside table. The way in, decided 5 Nov, is the lamp's own access point: a first boot with nothing stored raises Wakecue-XXXX, the phone opens a page served from the lamp, and what it types is stored and then used to join the house network. That makes NTP part of the product rather than a bench convenience: with no cell fitted yet, every unplug loses the time, and the network is the only thing that can put it back. The cost is known and accepted — esp-radio 1.0.0-beta.1 wants alloc and an async executor, so the image leaves its current 129 KB behind, against 1 MB of application partition. None of this is written yet; it is the direction, and the build log above is where it will show up once it runs. What survives unchanged is the rule the bench produced: nothing about the network may sit on the path to sleep, so any exchange has a deadline and its failure means “no fresh time”, never “no sleep”, and a wake with nothing to fetch still never starts the radio — the OSF flag decides that, which is what keeps the minute tick a flicker.
  • Generating the project: with esp-generate 2.0.0 the chip is chosen with -o esp32c3, not --chip.
  • The clock's OSF flag decides whether the time can be trusted. Losing power latches bit 7 of STATUS (0x0F), and only writing a 0 clears it. With no cell fitted, every unplug sets it. So boot reads STATUS first: OSF set means the time in the registers is not valid and has to be written (NTP on the bench, the user later); OSF clear means it is. It does not affect Alarm 1, which compares against whatever the registers hold.
  • Deep sleep takes the USB port with it, so getting back in has its own routine. The C3’s USB-Serial-JTAG is a peripheral, and sleeping switches it off, so cargo run’s monitor ends with “no serial ports” or “broken pipe” the moment the chip sleeps, and the board is only reachable during the awake window. Flash with --after watchdog-reset, because the default DTR/RTS reset leaves the chip in download mode over USB-JTAG (boot:0x7 … waiting for download). That setting cannot be combined with --monitor, though: the watchdog reset starts the app, whose USB-Serial-JTAG re-enumerates, and the monitor dies holding a dead port with I/O error after a flash that actually succeeded. So the recipe that reaches a sleeping board, just flash, leaves the monitor off; just lamp is for a board that is already awake. Reaching a sleeping board takes holding BOOT while the USB cable goes in, then releasing it; the usual hold BOOT, tap RST, release BOOT did not take on this SuperMini (tried 5 Nov). That unplug is not free — the clock has no cell, so it restarts at 2000 and sets OSF again, and the time has to be written once more. Hence DEV_MODE in src/bin/main.rs: with it true the boot runs for real and then opens the menu window without needing a tap and without ever closing it — the buttons, the pad, the menu and the clock's own answer, the port never drops, and the next flash is a plain just lamp. Read logs with espflash monitor -L defmt, because the monitor decodes plain text unless told otherwise.
  • Every wake is a reboot, so nothing survives in RAM. The boot path is therefore: read STATUS (is OSF set?), read the time, compute the next event, program Alarm 1, clear A1F, sleep. Consuming an alarm and then sleeping without programming the next one means sleeping until someone presses a button.
  • A wake pin must idle away from its wake level, and the firmware checks it. A pin already sitting at its wake level when the sleep starts ends that sleep at once, and the board then looks like it cannot sleep at all. That is why the two pins take opposite levels — the clock's open-drain SQW is armed low, the pad's driven OUT is armed high — and why sleep entry first waits until both levels are clear, naming the pin that is holding them.
